01 Name and one-liner

Seattle has 646 public traffic cameras nobody watches. We watch all of them, every minute, and route you around what they see. the sentence a judge repeats to another judge

The product claim is deliberately narrow: we report what a camera can show and what the collision record says. We never output a safety verdict. That restraint is what makes the claim defensible under grilling, and it is enforced in the model prompt itself.

02 Track and fit

See. Continuous ingest from 646 fixed cameras, a vision-language model reading every frame into structured observations, and a spatial index that ties each read to a street segment. The VLM output is the product — delete it and this is a CSV join. That test is the reason this is a See project and not a Do project with a camera bolted on.

03 Problem and who cares

The user is a person on foot in a downtown they do not know: a conference attendee walking back to a hotel after dark, a night-shift worker leaving a shift at 11pm, a visitor deciding between two routes that look identical on a map. Every mapping app they own optimises for arrival time. None of them can answer the question actually being asked, which is what does that street look like right now.

The gap is real and measurable. Seattle's own collision record holds 5,648 pedestrian and cyclist collisions in the downtown core, and they are not evenly spread — 2nd Ave between Union and Pike alone accounts for 60. The city already owns cameras watching 48% of those blocks. Nobody has ever connected the two.

04 The demo

0:00 — opening state

The map is already on screen, drawn from our own street geometry, no tile server. A judge picks two points from a dropdown: Westlake Center to Pioneer Square.

0:25 — the comparison

Two routes draw instantly. Not a score — a trade:

Walking 74 m further — about one extra minute — routes you past 34 fewer recorded pedestrian collisions and 5 fewer serious or fatal ones. computed live from 52,464 collision records in 2.4 ms

1:00 — the money moment

Below the map, the route redraws as a contact sheet: the actual camera frames along the walk, in walking order, each with the model's read underneath and a bar showing how that corner compares to the rest of downtown at this moment. One route in testing spanned p17 to p99 — the same fifteen-minute walk passing both the quietest and the busiest blocks in the city. Judges are not looking at a dashboard, they are looking at their own city, ten minutes ago, annotated. Someone in the room will recognise an intersection.

1:40 — the beat we did not script

At 10:20 on Saturday the model flagged 9th Ave & Pine St: traffic stopped, sidewalk blocked, construction true, and the note "the street is closed and pedestrians are being directed around the area." We pulled the frame. There are barricades across the road and a sign that reads STREET CLOSED.

Nobody staged this. It is the product doing its job on a random Saturday morning, and it is the single most convincing thing we can put on a screen. If something comparable is live at 3pm Sunday we lead with it; if not, we show this one and say plainly that it is from Saturday.

2:00 — the live beat

A judge names any intersection. We click it. The frame is fetched from the city right now and read on the spot. One camera, live, roughly twelve seconds on the laptop — and that twelve seconds is the setup for section 5.

If the wifi dies

The demo does not change. Street geometry, routing graph, collision layers, camera frames, model weights and the entire read corpus are on local disk. There is no tile server, no CDN, no external font, no cloud API anywhere in the path. The only thing that degrades is the single live fetch, which falls back to the last stored frame with a visible staleness badge. We can unplug the network mid-demo and keep talking.

05 Why the Spark

We do not argue this. We measure it.

Full 646-camera sweep2.1 hoursunder 1 minute
Data freshness that impliesstale before it finishesactually live
A city-wide sweep takes two hours on a laptop, so the answer is history by the time you get it. On the Spark it is under a minute, so it is the present tense. Same code, one environment variable. the answer to "why isn't this a GPT-wrapper"

The specific capabilities being used: 128 GB unified memory holding the VLM, an embedding model and the report model resident together rather than swapping; strong vision prefill, which is exactly the shape of this workload since latency here is prefill-bound, not decode-bound (measured — cutting output tokens made it slower); and continuous ingest across hundreds of streams.

The cloud alternative is also genuinely bad here: 646 cameras at one-minute refresh is 930,000 vision calls a day. That is not a rounding error on anyone's API bill.

ARM64 check

Dev machine is Apple ARM, the box is ARM — the port direction is favourable. Verified working on ARM today: mlx, mlx-vlm, shapely, networkx, fastapi, httpx, pillow. Not yet verified on aarch64 CUDA: the inference backend swap itself — MLX is Apple-only, so the box needs vLLM or TensorRT-LLM behind the same infer() interface. This is the one real porting task and it is deliberately isolated to a single function.

One trap worth recording: the dev machine shipped Python 3.14, which has no wheels for this stack. Pin 3.12.

Acquisition is done. Current corpus on disk: 14,698 frames, 33 sweeps, 1.5 GB, growing continuously since 01:30. Retention keeps recent frames per camera and thins the rest to hourly, so the footprint stays bounded.

Three data traps we already hit

  1. Dead cameras return HTTP 200 with a "camera under maintenance" JPEG, not a 404. Detected by learning hashes that appear on several different cameras at once — a real street view is never byte-identical to another.
  2. The feed clusters by zoom level. At zoomId=13 every camera in a cluster reports the cluster centroid, so 22 downtown cameras shared one coordinate and every spatial join was silently fiction. At zoomId=18 coverage went from 199 to 824 segments. This bug produced confident, plausible, completely wrong numbers — it is the reason section 5 quotes only things we re-measured after the fix.
  3. Downscaling breaks the model. Below 1024 px the VLM called a 2 a.m. street "daylight". Verified against the pixels by hand. Resolution stays up, and the latency cost is the honest price.

Fallback if the feed goes down

We already hold a growing local archive. A demo run entirely from stored frames is indistinguishable except for the staleness badge.

GRILL The card — all five of us give these answers

Teams lose the grill by contradicting each other, not by lacking answers.

"Why isn't this a GPT-wrapper on a laptop?"

A full city sweep is 2.1 hours on our laptop and under a minute on the box. We measured both. Below that threshold the data is live; above it, it's history. Also, 646 cameras at one-minute refresh is 930,000 vision calls a day against a cloud API.

"Isn't this just a dashboard on open data?"

The camera feed isn't in the open data portal — it's a clustered map endpoint we had to reverse-engineer. And we don't output a score; we output the actual frames. The evidence is the product.

"Did you build a redlining machine?"

No, and deliberately. We never touch crime data. Our inputs are the collision record, the sidewalk inventory, street grade, and what a camera can currently show. The model is prompt-forbidden from producing a safety verdict — it reports observations, the user judges. We can show you the prompt.

"What if the model is hallucinating?"

Show the dawn chart. Then volunteer the error rate: 5 of 2,345 reads called August "snow".

"Does it work for another city?"

The camera feed is Seattle-specific; the pipeline is not. Swap the inventory adapter and the ArcGIS layer names. We scoped to one city on purpose rather than demo two badly.

"What's pre-computed?"

The read corpus. Routing and the named-intersection read are live. Say it before being asked.

14 Open questions

  • Does the box exist for us? Everything is written to survive it not arriving, but the Spark-necessity score depends on proving the port.
  • What does Cosmos-Reason1-7B do on these frames? Untested. Potentially better at physical-scene reasoning than Qwen, and it is NVIDIA's own model at an NVIDIA event.
  • Resolved, and worth recording how. We built the time-of-day baseline and then rejected its headline claim. With ten hours of one day, a camera's "own baseline" is dominated by 3 a.m. readings, so at 10 a.m. almost every corner reads as "busier than usual" — a signal that sounds insightful and carries no information. We replaced it with city-relative ranking: every camera scored against every other camera in the same sweep. Sun, weather and day-of-week are shared by the whole city and cancel out, leaving only the difference between this corner and the rest of downtown right now. It needs no history at all, which is why it is the version that ships.
  • Is 3pm daylight fatal to the wow? The night frames are dramatically more compelling. Open question whether to lead with a labelled cached night route.
